Supplemental Experimental Procedures
ChIP experiments in COLO829
ChIP-seq experiments were performed as previously described (Wilson et al., 2010). In brief, early passage cells
were cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde and nuclei extracted and lysed. Chromatin was sonicated for 6 hours in a
Diagenode (Wallonia, Belgium) Pico Bioruptor (30 seconds on/ 30 seconds off) and divided across two samples,
incubated with polyclonal rabbit anti-CTCF antibody (Millipore 07-729) and rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG;
Sigma I5006) respectively. Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and used for library construction, followed
by sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (BGI, Hong Kong). Two independent ChIP experiments
using COLO829 cells grown to different passages and harvested independently were performed and sequenced
separately. Replicate 1 was performed using ~20 million cells for each condition (CTCF and IgG) and replicate
2 was performed using ~40 million cells for each condition.
DNA-affinity experiments in COLO829
For DNA-affinity experiments, genomic DNA was isolated from COLO829 as previously described (Poulos et
al., 2015) and a region (chr17:48,758,701-48,758,900) isolated for DNA baits using the following primers –
forward: CTCCTTCACCGTTGTCTC; and reverse: CTGGAATGGAAGATGTGATG. The PCR product,
representing both wild-type and mutant alleles, was cloned into the pGEM®-T Easy Vector System per
manufacturer’s instructions (Promega Corporation, WI, USA). Wild-type and mutant plasmid clones were
selected and purified (Figure S2d). To produce wild-type and mutant DNA baits, the region was PCR amplified
from wild-type and mutant plasmids respectively, using a 5’ biotinylated forward primer so that each DNA bait
had a biotin tag. PCR reactions were pooled to produce a sufficient quantity of DNA baits. The integrity of the
PCR product was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.
1.5 µg of dsDNA baits were loaded with 300 µg of Streptavidin dynabeads (M-280; Thermo Fisher Scientific,
NSW, Australia) and kept at room temperature for 3 hours. After incubation with a blocking buffer, DNAconjugated beads were incubated with pre-cleared COLO829 cell lysate which had been extracted using the NEPER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents per kit instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NSW,
Australia). Each 300 µg of DNA-conjugated bead condition was incubated with 0.2 mg of pre-cleared nuclear
protein extract from COLO829, in the presence of 10 µM zinc from zinc acetate, overnight at 4 oC. The bound
proteins were boiled off the beads in 4x NuPAGE LD buffer (NP0007; Life Technologies, VIC, Australia)
supplemented with 200 mM DTT at 95oC for 10 minutes. Equal volumes of elute from each DNA bait, and an
aliquot of the protein input were loaded in separate lanes and separated on a 4-12% Bis-Tris gel (Life
Technologies, VIC, Australia), and then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Western blotting was
performed using 5% skim milk as a blocking reagent, polyclonal rabbit anti-CTCF antibody (Millipore 07-729)
as the primary antibody and polyclonal goat anti-rabbit IgG (Dako P0448) as the secondary antibody. A silver
stain was performed on the gel following transfer to the nitrocellulose membrane to serve as a loading control
(Figure S2e). The silver stain was performed using reagents from Pierce Silver Stain for Mass Spectrometry per
kit instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NSW, Australia).
Variant allele frequency and copy number variation analysis
Variant allele frequencies (VAFs) in the melanoma samples were calculated by determining base calls at
specific sites using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009) mpileup on the BAM files obtained from CGHub (Wilks et al.,
2014). Reads were disregarded if they recorded a base call which was neither the wild-type nor mutant
nucleotide (per the mutation calls made by Strelka (Saunders et al., 2012)). The VAF was calculated as the
proportion of mutant base calls, over the total number of mutant and wild-type base calls, from all reads
covering a given nucleotide.
Copy number segment information data for melanoma sample TCGA-D9-A148 was obtained from the Genomic
Data Commons (GDC).
ChIP-sequencing data analysis
The NHEK CTCF ChIP-seq peaks used for analyses in this study were those from the processed ‘narrow peak’
BED files obtained directly from ENCODE. Peaks from the two replicates were merged to provide a complete
list. To ensure that all regions used were of the same size, for all regions greater than 150 bp after merging, the
centre 150 bp was used. To determine the CTCF read count from ChIP-seq across a given region, the

corresponding BAM files were used. BEDtools (Quinlan and Hall, 2010) was used to convert the BAM to BED
files and extend reads downstream to 150 bp. The two replicates were then combined for further analysis.
COLO829 ChIP-seq data were obtained in fastq format and aligned using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (Li
and Durbin, 2009). The resultant SAM file was converted to BAM format and sorted using SAMtools (Li et al.,
2009). Peaks were called using the Homer Suite (Heinz et al., 2010) with ‘factor’ as the style option and IgG as
the background control. Only CTCF peaks present across replicate experiments were used for analysis. To
ensure standard peak sizes (332 bp by default), where peaks overlapped, peaks from the first replicate
experiment were kept.
To calculate the normalised variant allele frequency (normVAF) of each COLO829 mutation from ChIP-seq
data, the reads from both ChIP-seq replicate experiments were combined. Base calls at specific sites were
determined using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009) mpileup on sorted BAM files of either COLO829 CTCF ChIP-seq
or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data. Reads were disregarded if they recorded a base call which was
neither the wild-type nor mutant nucleotide, using base calls as previously recorded (Craig et al., 2016).
Normalisation for copy number variation and mapability was performed by using COLO829 WGS read
coverage to calculate the normVAF, making use of the following formula:

Repair dataset processing
Genome-wide nucleotide excision repair (NER) sequencing (excision repair sequencing; XR-seq) cyclobutane
pyrimidine dimer (CPD) repair data from UV light-irradiated normal human skin fibroblast (NHF) cells (Hu et
al., 2015) were obtained in Sequence Read Archive (SRA) format (GEO: GSM1659156). As previously
described (Hu et al., 2015), the raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014) and aligned
using Bowtie (Langmead et al., 2009). De-duplicated aligned reads for the two replicate experiments were
merged for analysis, and reads were discarded if less than 20 bp in length. Time-course CPD and (64)pyrimidine-pyrimidone photoproduct ((6-4)PP) data XR-seq data for NHF cells for all time points (Adar et al.,
2016) were obtained in SRA format (GEO: GSE76391). Reads were processed as described above, but with
reads discarded only if they were greater than 36 bp in length. This is because reads greater than this length are
unlikely to be nucleotide excision fragments which are generally 22-30 nucleotides in length (Hu et al., 2015).
CTCF motif identification
The strict 13 bp CTCF motifs (n=5,470; Table S1a) were confined to only those motifs which adhered to the
following sequence CCA(C|G)(C|T)AG(A|G)(G|T)GGC(A|G) which overlapped with any of the 150 bp merged
list of peaks from the replicate CTCF ChIP-seq datasets from NHEK cells. We defined the control motif as
consisting of any region overlapping a CTCF NHEK ChIP-seq peak with the same six bases at the 3’ end of the
motif as those in the strict 13 bp CTCF motif, but with any combination of bases at the 5’ end of the motif, as
follows: NNNNNNNN(A|G)(G|T)GGC(A|G), where N represents any nucleotide. Control motifs were excluded
if the six bases at the 3’ end of the motif overlapped with any full motifs identified previously, with a final count
of n=80,551 control motifs identified. A subset of n=5,470 control motifs (Table S1b) were randomly selected
from the full list such that they were matched for replication timing with the strict CTCF motif (that is,
corresponding motifs had replication timing within ± 1 of each corresponding strict CTCF motif).
To identify mutations in CTCF motifs in COLO829, motifs were identified within CTCF peaks from COLO829
CTCF ChIP-seq (only peaks common between replicate experiments were used, as described above). CTCF
motifs were identified using the consensus CTCF motif from the JASPAR database (Mathelier et al., 2015),
using FIMO (Grant et al., 2011) with default settings.
For the analyses of NHEK CTCF motifs, motifs were identified within CTCF peaks from NHEK CTCF ChIPseq (the merged list of 150 bp peaks was used for this analysis, as described above). NHEK CTCF motifs
(n=56,009) were identified using the consensus CTCF motif from the JASPAR database (Mathelier et al., 2015),
using FIMO (Grant et al., 2011) with default settings.

motifs within TAD anchors from NHEK cells (Rao et al., 2014)), and those that fell into non-anchor regions
(n=3,883; all remaining motifs).
Data binning in Figure 2
Binned data is shown in Figure 2b and Figure 2e. In Figure 2b, for motifs with RAD21 binding, data was
binned by CTCF reads across the motif, incrementally increasing by 25, with all motifs with ≥ 600 CTCF reads
allocated to the final bin. For motifs without RAD21 binding, data was binned by CTCF reads across the motif,
incrementally increasing by 10, with all motifs with ≥ 90 CTCF reads allocated to the final bin. In Figure 2e,
data was allocated to seven equally-sized bins, with bins ranging from replication timing of 10 to 80 (Q1 ChIPseq) and replication timing of 15 to 85 (Q4 ChIP-seq). Within bins, CTCF reads (Figure 2b) or replication
timing (Figure 2e) were averaged to provide the y-axis value, and mutations were averaged and normalised to
mutations per Mb per sample to provide the x-axis value.
Mutation simulation
Calculation of the expected distribution of mutations across CTCF binding sites utilised mutations randomly
shuffled across the genome using “shuffle” from BEDtools (Quinlan and Hall, 2010), across 1,000 iterations.
Simulated mutations within the CTCF motifs were calculated by adopting trinucleotide mutation signatures as
described (Alexandrov et al., 2013). Trinucleotide mutation signatures for each cancer type were determined by
combining all mutations within a cancer type, and counting the number of each of the possible 96 trinucleotides.
Mutations were then randomly allocated across the genome to locations with the corresponding trinucleotide,
according to the number of each trinucleotide mutated within each cancer type. The mutation density was then
calculated at each base of the CTCF motif using these randomly allocated mutations. This process was repeated
for a total of 1,000 iterations, and the average mutation density at each base within the CTCF motif was
calculated.
Bootstrapping analysis
Bootstrapping for percentage of cancer-associated genes (Figure 4a) was performed by selecting all TAD
anchor motif mutations from melanoma samples (using TAD anchor motifs described above) and then randomly
shuffling these mutations across all anchor motifs. All genes with a TSS within a gene loop with a mutated
anchor motif were then identified, and the proportion of these that were cancer-associated was calculated. This
process was repeated through 1,000 iterations to obtain robust confidence intervals around the mean proportion
of cancer-associated genes from random iterations.
Bootstrapping for percentage of cancer-associated (Figure S3b) and non-cancer associated (Figure S3c) genes
within unique anchor-gene-loop groupings was performed as follows. All TAD anchor motif mutations in
melanoma samples were identified, as described above. These mutations were then randomly shuffled across all
anchor motifs, and all genes with TSSs within loops with mutated anchor motifs were identified. All unique
anchor-gene-loop groupings were identified, and expression differences from melanoma RNA-sequencing data
were calculated as described above, using wild-type and mutant melanoma sample designations from the
randomly shuffled mutations. Gene expression differences were identified either by unpaired t-test or unpaired
one-sample t-test, whichever was appropriate. Multiple testing correction was then performed using the
Bonferroni correction method on all P-values obtained, and the proportion of significantly differentiallyexpressed genes within each iteration after multiple testing correction was determined, using a threshold of P <

0.05 to determine significance. A distribution of results was calculated by repeating this process through 1,000
iterations.
